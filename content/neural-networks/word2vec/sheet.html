The problem: words as atomic units

In 2013 most NLP systems treat a word as an atomic unit — an index into a vocabulary, equivalently a one-hot vector: V positions, all zero, a single 1. The representation is simple and robust, but it carries no notion of similarity: every pair of distinct words is exactly equally different. hotel is as unrelated to motel as it is to algebra — whatever a model learns about one word teaches it nothing about its neighbours.

It persisted because it worked: simple N-gram models — plain next-word statistics counted from text — trained on huge corpora kept beating cleverer models trained on less. The lesson the field had absorbed was scale wins.

Distributed representations were not new — neural language models had been producing them as a side effect for a decade. But none had been trained on more than a few hundred million words, at a modest 50–100 dimensions. The paper's stated goal is the scale jump: learn high-quality vectors from billions of words and a millions-strong vocabulary — and to judge "quality" by something sharper than "similar words end up close": the expectation that a good space encodes relations as linear regularities, computable with plain vector arithmetic. That bet is cashed out in the geometry section below.

Where vectors come from: predict a neighbour

There are no labels anywhere in this story. Raw text supervises itself: hide a word, ask the model to predict it from the words around it. Any neural model doing that job must first map each word to a vector — a projection matrix with one row of D numbers per vocabulary word. Those rows are the word vectors. The prediction task is the means; the matrix it leaves behind is the product.

Why prediction manufactures similarity: the model's output is computed from the vector, so two words used in interchangeable contexts (hotel/motel) can only earn similarly good predictions by taking nearby vectors. Similarity of use becomes proximity in space — nobody ever tells the model that hotels resemble motels; the objective forces it.

The two existing architectures the paper starts from, both trained exactly this way:

  • NNLM (feed-forward, Bengio-style) — concatenate the vectors of the N previous words (N = 10), pass them through a nonlinear hidden layer of H units (500–1000), then a softmax scoring every vocabulary word as the possible next word.
  • RNNLM (recurrent) — no projection window; a recurrent hidden state carries short-term memory of everything read so far, then the same softmax.

Both produce good vectors. Both are far too slow to train on billions of words — and saying precisely where the time goes is the paper's actual contribution.

The cost lens: O = E × T × Q

The paper puts one formula over every architecture: total training cost O = E × T × Q — epochs (E = 3–50) × words in the corpus (T, up to 10⁹) × per-word cost Q. E and T are fixed by the ambition; only Q is negotiable. The whole design fight happens inside Q:

  • NNLM: Q = N×D + N×D×H + H×V — projection, hidden layer, output softmax.
  • RNNLM: Q = H×H + H×V — recurrence, output softmax.

Two terms are monsters, and they die differently:

TermWhat it isIts fate
H×Vthe output softmax — to train one prediction, score every vocabulary word, all million of themsolved by a tree. Arrange the vocabulary as a binary tree, one leaf per word; a word's probability is the product of the binary choices along its root-to-leaf path, so one prediction evaluates log₂(V) nodes (~20) instead of V (10⁶). The paper uses a Huffman tree — frequent words get short paths — cutting the effective cost to ~log₂(unigram perplexity), about another 2× at V = 1M.
N×D×Hthe nonlinear hidden layer — the model's actual intelligencedeleted. This is the paper's whole move: accept a much simpler model, and spend the savings on orders of magnitude more data. Scale wins — the N-gram lesson, applied to neural nets.

With the softmax tamed by the tree, the hidden layer stood exposed as the true bottleneck — and everything that remains after deleting it is a projection matrix and a tree softmax. Models with no hidden layer, whose output is a linear function of the (log-)inputs, are called log-linear. The paper proposes two.

Two log-linear models: CBOW and skip-gram

Both keep only the shared projection matrix (the word vectors themselves) and the hierarchical-softmax output. They differ in nothing but the direction of prediction:

CBOW — continuous bag-of-words. Predicts the current word from its context: best results with four history + four future words. Their vectors are averaged into one projection before predicting — word order vanishes, hence bag of words; continuous because the bag holds dense vectors, not counts. Q = N×D + D×log₂(V) — the fastest model in the paper.

Skip-gram. Reverses the arrow: the current word predicts the words around it, one classification per neighbour. At each position a number R is drawn at random in ⟨1;C⟩ and the model predicts R history + R future words — so distant words are sampled less often and weigh less. Experiments use C = 10. Q = C × (D + D×log₂(V)) — costlier than CBOW: up to 2R classifications per position.

The training loop — and the answer to "what does word2vec optimise": slide the window over all T words and maximise the (tree-)softmax probability of the prediction — P(w(t) | averaged context) for CBOW, P(w(t±j) | w(t)) for each sampled neighbour in skip-gram. Meaning as geometry: relations are directions

Training only ever asked "which word sits near which?" — yet the space that falls out encodes far more than proximity. Relations between words come out as directions, and plain vector arithmetic travels along them. The paper's own examples, answered by taking the nearest word by cosine similarity (angle between vectors — length ignored):

the paper's algebra — nearest word by cosine
v("biggest") − v("big") + v("small")  →  "smallest"
v("King") − v("Man") + v("Woman")     →  "Queen"

Why does this work? The paper demonstrates it empirically ("somewhat surprisingly") rather than proving it. The mechanism it leans on:

  • The model is log-linear: a word's predictions are a linear function of its vector. Interchangeable contexts therefore force nearby vectors — that much was already argued above.
  • A relation that shifts a word's contexts consistently (masculine↔feminine, verb↔past tense, country↔capital) must shift the vectors consistently too — a linear model has no other way to encode it. The relation becomes a near-constant offset, the same displacement wherever it applies.
  • That is what the paper's opening bet — words have multiple degrees of similarity — cashes out to: not one similarity score but many directions at once.

The analogy test set

To measure the regularities instead of admiring them, the paper builds a test of 8,869 semantic + 10,675 syntactic questions. Each is an analogy a : b :: c : ?, answered with the geometry recipe — compute v(b) − v(a) + v(c), take the nearest word by cosine — and scored harshly: it counts only if the nearest word is exactly the expected one; synonyms count as mistakes. No partial credit, so the numbers understate the quality of the space.

Results and scaling lessons

On identical data (320M words) at 640 dimensions, the two new models split the test between them — and both beat the NNLM and RNNLM baselines:

Model · 640dSemantic accuracySyntactic accuracy
CBOW24%64%
Skip-gram55%59%

Skip-gram owns semantics; CBOW owns syntax and trains fastest. The remaining experiments read like scaling laws before the term existed:

  • Dimensions and data must grow together. Adding either alone hits diminishing returns; doubling the data costs about the same compute as doubling the vector size.
  • More data beats more epochs. CBOW 300d: 783M words × 3 epochs and 1.6B words × 1 epoch both score 36.1% — but the single pass takes 0.6 days instead of 3. Sweep new text, don't re-read old text.
  • The scale jump delivered. Trained on the ~6B-token Google News corpus (vocabulary capped at the 1M most frequent words) with DistBelief — 50–100 model replicas, asynchronous mini-batch gradient descent, Adagrad — a 1000-dimensional skip-gram becomes the paper's headline model.
  • Beyond analogies: on the Microsoft Sentence Completion Challenge, skip-gram alone scores 48.0%, and mixed with RNNLM reaches 58.9% — a new state of the art at the time.

Edges of this paper

  • Not in here: negative sampling. This paper's output is an exact (hierarchical) softmax. Negative sampling, frequent-word subsampling and phrase vectors (New York as one token) arrive in the follow-up, Distributed Representations of Words and Phrases (NIPS 2013) — most "word2vec" explainers describe that paper, not this one.
  • The count-based contrast: GloVe (2014) reaches similar geometry from global co-occurrence counts instead of a sliding classifier.
  • The through-line to LLMs: every model later in the Plan starts with an embedding layer — the same projection matrix, trained jointly with the network instead of on its own. What survives from this paper is its founding assumption: meaning is a position in vector space, and relations are directions in it.
