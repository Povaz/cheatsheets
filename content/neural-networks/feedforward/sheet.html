1958: the perceptron

Rosenblatt's opening move is a position, not an algorithm: the connectionist (empiricist) bet that information is stored as new connections, not as coded records — so recognising a stimulus needs no separate lookup, the strengthened pathways simply fire. And because real nervous tissue is imperfect and largely random, he models it with probability theory rather than symbolic logic — against the exact-wiring tradition of McCulloch & Pitts.

The perceptron is that bet made concrete — a feedforward chain of threshold units:

  • S-points — sensory units responding all-or-nothing to the stimulus.
  • A-units — association cells; each fires when the algebraic sum of its excitatory and inhibitory inputs reaches its threshold θ. Their origin points are scattered at random — the first layer is wiring, not learning.
  • R-units — responses, fed by a random source-set of A-units.
  • Learning — each A-unit carries a value V: active units that fire when a response is reinforced gain value, so the response's source-set gradually dominates. No error is computed; frequency and reinforcement do the work.

Rosenblatt's own conclusions already sound modern: such a system learns to associate responses to stimuli in a differentiated environment, generalises to unseen stimuli of a learned class (statistical separability), and its memory is distributed — removing part of the association layer degrades everything a little, nothing completely.

The wall: no hidden layer, no XOR

A network whose only trainable stage maps inputs straight to outputs has one essential character: similar inputs produce similar outputs — similarity being input overlap, fixed by the outside world. Whenever the task needs the opposite — the classic case is XOR, where the two least-overlapping patterns (00, 11) demand the same answer — no such network can learn it.

  • Minsky & Papert (1969) analysed exactly which mappings these one-stage learners can and cannot do — and the cannot-list is long.
  • The escape was known even then: add a layer of hidden units between input and output, and some recoding of the inputs (an internal representation) always exists that makes the mapping learnable — e.g. one extra feature "both inputs on" turns XOR solvable.
  • The missing piece was a learning rule for the hidden layer: the perceptron trained only its final connections; nobody could assign credit to units with no specified target.

1986: backpropagation — the generalized delta rule

Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams close that gap. Make every unit's activation a differentiable function of its weighted input (they use the logistic squashing function instead of a hard threshold), define the error E as the summed squared difference between outputs and targets, and gradient descent gives a rule for every weight — hidden ones included:

The generalized delta rule each weight moves by  Δw = η · δ · o  (error signal of the receiver × output of the sender)

output units:  δⱼ = (tⱼ − oⱼ) · f′(netⱼ)
hidden units:  δⱼ = f′(netⱼ) · Σₖ δₖ·wₖⱼ — recursively, from the error signals of the units it feeds
forwardpropagate input → compute every o
comparetargets give δ at the outputs
backwardpass δ layer by layer through the weights
updateΔw = η·δ·o (+ momentum term α)
  • Symmetry breaking — identical initial weights would stay identical forever, so weights start small and random.
  • Local minima — gradient descent has no guarantee, but across hundreds of problems the authors found genuine local minima very rare.
  • The payoff named in the title: hidden units learn internal representations — the network invents the features (like XOR's "both on") that the input encoding lacked. Training hidden layers is what turns the perceptron into the modern feedforward network.

Why word2vec cites this

  • The NNLM that word2vec measures itself against is exactly this machine: projection layer → hidden layer of nonlinear units → softmax output, trained end-to-end by backpropagation. Its cost term N×D×H — the one word2vec deletes — is the hidden layer this page introduced.
  • The transformer keeps the species alive: its position-wise FFN is a two-layer feedforward network, backprop-trained, inside every block.
  • The two papers split the concept cleanly: 1958 gives the shape (layers of weighted-sum threshold units, learning in the connections, distributed memory) and 1986 gives the training (differentiable units, error backpropagated through hidden layers).
