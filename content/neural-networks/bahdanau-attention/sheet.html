The bottleneck: one vector per sentence

2014 neural machine translation is the encoder–decoder (seq2seq): an encoder RNN reads the source sentence and compresses it into one fixed-length vector c; a decoder RNN unrolls the translation from c alone. Trained end-to-end, single system — a clean break from phrase-based pipelines like Moses.

The paper's target is the compression step itself: everything the decoder will ever know about the source must survive the squeeze into c — a 20-word sentence and a 60-word sentence get the same budget. Cho et al. had just measured the consequence: the performance of a basic encoder–decoder deteriorates rapidly as input sentences grow longer.

The idea: learn to align and translate, jointly

Keep one vector per source word instead of one per sentence, and let the decoder (soft-)search them at every output step:

  • The encoder emits a sequence of annotations h₁ … hₜ, one per source word.
  • At each output step the decoder scores every annotation for relevance, and builds a fresh context vector cᵢ — a different mix of the source for every target word.
  • This relieves the encoder from the burden of encoding the whole sentence into a fixed-length vector; information stays spread out, and is retrieved selectively. The paper's own phrase for the retrieval: the decoder decides which parts of the source to pay attention to.

Alignment — which source words produce which target word — is classic MT machinery, but here it is not a latent variable: the alignment is soft, computed directly by a small network, so it is differentiable and gradients backpropagate straight through it. Translation and alignment train together, one loss.

Names to remember from the experiments: RNNencdec (the fixed-vector baseline) vs RNNsearch (the proposed model).

RNNsearch, piece by piece

Encoder — bidirectional RNN. Two RNNs with gated hidden units (the GRU of Cho et al.) read the sentence forwards and backwards; word j's annotation concatenates both states: hⱼ = [→hⱼ ; ←hⱼ]. Each annotation therefore summarises the whole sentence, focused around word j.

Decoder. The state update and the next-word distribution both consume the per-step context cᵢ:

Decoder · one step i state  sᵢ = f(sᵢ₋₁, yᵢ₋₁, cᵢ)
output  p(yᵢ | y₁…yᵢ₋₁, x) = g(yᵢ₋₁, sᵢ, cᵢ)

Experiment scale: 1000 hidden units in encoder and decoder, 620-dimensional word embeddings, a maxout output layer of 500.

The attention computation

The retrieval is three moves — score, normalise, mix:

1 · score — the alignment model (a one-hidden-layer MLP, trained jointly) eᵢⱼ = vₐᵀ · tanh(Wₐ·sᵢ₋₁ + Uₐ·hⱼ) — how well does source position j match output step i?
2 · normalise — softmax over source positions αᵢⱼ = exp(eᵢⱼ) / Σₖ exp(eᵢₖ) — the weights sum to 1; read αᵢⱼ as the probability that yᵢ aligns to xⱼ.
3 · mix — expected annotation cᵢ = Σⱼ αᵢⱼ · hⱼ — the context is the annotations averaged under those probabilities.

Everything here is differentiable, so the whole system — alignment model, encoder, decoder — trains as one network on the translation loss. This scoring form (add the two projections, tanh, dot with vₐ) is what later work calls additive attention.

Did it work?

English→French, WMT '14, 348M words of parallel text, vocabulary capped at the 30,000 most frequent words per language (rest → UNK). Models trained on sentences up to 30 or up to 50 words, ~5 days each with minibatch SGD + Adadelta, decoded by beam search. BLEU on the test set:

  • Attention beats the bottleneck at every size — and the gap widens with sentence length: RNNsearch-50 shows no deterioration even at 50+ words, where RNNencdec collapses.
  • Parity with a mature SMT system on sentences without unknown words — a first for pure neural translation.
  • The weights are inspectable: plotting αᵢⱼ gives per-sentence alignment maps — largely monotonic, with correct reorderings (European Economic Area → zone économique européenne aligns zone↔Area across two words). Soft weights also share credit naturally: l' in l'homme attends to both the and man — no word maps to or from nowhere.

Legacy: attention, three years early

  • What was born here is the mechanism, not the acronym-free math it later got: attention arrived as a fix for an RNN bottleneck in translation, not as a general sequence layer.
  • The template survived intact — score every candidate, softmax, weighted sum. Reading forward: the annotations hⱼ play the role later split into keys and values, the decoder state sᵢ₋₁ is the query, and the additive MLP score is the part Attention Is All You Need (2017) swaps for a scaled dot product — then deletes the RNNs around it entirely.
  • Two habits of modern practice debut here too: differentiable soft selection instead of latent hard choices, and reading model internals (αᵢⱼ maps) as an interpretability tool.
