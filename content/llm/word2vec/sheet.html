The problem: words as atomic units

In 2013 most NLP systems treat words as atomic units — indices into a vocabulary. Simple and robust (and N-gram models on huge data beat clever models on less), but an index carries no notion of similarity: hotel is exactly as unrelated to motel as it is to algebra.

The alternative is a distributed representation: each word is a dense vector of D real numbers, its meaning spread across the dimensions and learned from raw text, not designed. Neural language models had already produced such vectors as a side effect — but none had been trained on more than a few hundred million words, at a modest 50–100 dimensions.

The paper's goal is exactly that scale jump: learn high-quality word vectors from billions of words with millions of vocabulary entries, and judge quality by something sharper than "similar words end up close":

The bet the paper makes Words have multiple degrees of similarity, and a good vector space encodes them as linear regularities — relations you can compute with plain vector arithmetic (§geometry). The models are designed to preserve these regularities, cheaply.

The cost framing: O = E × T × Q

The paper's lens on every architecture is its training cost:

Training complexity O = E × T × Q — epochs (E = 3–50) × words in the corpus (T up to 10⁹) × per-word cost Q of the model. Only Q is negotiable — so the whole design fight happens there.

The two predecessors, and where their Q goes:

  • NNLM (feed-forward, Bengio-style) — N previous words (N = 10), each projected to D dimensions by a shared matrix, a nonlinear hidden layer of H units (500–1000), softmax over the vocabulary V: Q = N×D + N×D×H + H×V.
  • RNNLM (recurrent) — no projection layer; a recurrent hidden state gives short-term memory: Q = H×H + H×V.

Killing the output term: hierarchical softmax

The raw softmax term H×V is huge, but it is a solved problem: arrange the vocabulary as a binary tree and classify along a root-to-leaf path — V drops to log₂(V). The paper uses a Huffman tree, which gives frequent words short codes: cost ~log₂(unigram perplexity), about 2× faster than a balanced tree at V = 1M.

With the output fixed, the true bottleneck stands exposed: the nonlinear hidden layer (N×D×H). The paper's whole move is to delete it — accept a simpler model, and spend the savings on orders of magnitude more data.

Two log-linear models: CBOW and Skip-gram

Both new architectures keep only a shared projection matrix (the word vectors themselves) and a hierarchical-softmax output — no hidden layer, hence log-linear. They differ only in the direction of prediction:

CBOW — continuous bag-of-words

  • Predicts the current word from its context: best results with a log-linear classifier fed four history + four future words.
  • The context vectors are averaged into one projection — word order vanishes, hence bag-of-words; continuous because the bag holds dense vectors, not counts.
  • Q = N×D + D×log₂(V) — the fastest model in the paper.

Skip-gram

  • Reverses the arrow: the current word predicts the words around it, one classification per neighbour.
  • For each word draw R at random in ⟨1;C⟩ and predict R history + R future words — distant words are sampled less often, so they weigh less. Experiments use C = 10.
  • Q = C × (D + D×log₂(V)) — costlier than CBOW: up to 2R training pairs per position.
What word2vec optimises — the exit check There are no labels; raw text supervises itself. Both models slide a window over the corpus and maximise the (hierarchical-)softmax probability of a word given its neighbours: CBOW maximises P(w(t) | averaged context), skip-gram maximises P(w(t±j) | w(t)) for each sampled neighbour. The word vectors are the projection weights this training leaves behind.

Meaning as geometry: vector arithmetic

Training only ever asked "which word sits near which?" — yet the resulting space encodes far more than proximity. Relations between words come out as directions, and simple algebra travels along them:

The paper's own examples X = vector("biggest") − vector("big") + vector("small") → the word closest to X by cosine distance is smallest.
vector("King") − vector("Man") + vector("Woman") → closest to Queen.

Why does this work? The paper demonstrates it empirically ("somewhat surprisingly") rather than proving it. The mechanism it leans on:

  • The model is log-linear: a word's predictions are a linear function of its vector. Two words used in interchangeable contexts can only earn similar predictions by taking nearby vectors — similarity of use becomes proximity.
  • A relation that shifts a word's contexts consistently (masculine↔feminine, country↔capital, adjective↔superlative) must therefore shift the vectors consistently too: the relation is stored as a near-constant offset, the same displacement wherever it applies.
  • That is what "multiple degrees of similarity" (§problem) cashes out to: one space, many simultaneous directions — number, gender, tense, geography — each usable by arithmetic.

The analogy test set

To measure the regularities instead of admiring them, the paper builds a test of 8,869 semantic + 10,675 syntactic questions. Each is an analogy a : b :: c : ?, answered with the §geometry recipe: compute vector(b) − vector(a) + vector(c), take the nearest word by cosine — and it counts only if it is exactly the expected word; synonyms count as mistakes.

Results and scaling lessons

On identical data (320M words) at 640 dimensions, the two new models split the test between them — and both log-linear models beat the NNLM and RNNLM baselines:

Model · 640dSemantic accuracySyntactic accuracy
CBOW24%64%
Skip-gram55%59%

Skip-gram owns semantics; CBOW owns syntax and trains fastest. The rest of the experiments read like scaling laws before the term existed:

  • Dimensions and data must grow together. Adding either alone hits diminishing returns; doubling the data costs about the same compute as doubling the vector size.
  • More data beats more epochs. CBOW 300d: 783M words × 3 epochs and 1.6B words × 1 epoch both score 36.1% — but the single pass takes 0.6 days instead of 3.
  • The scale jump delivered. Trained on the ~6B-token Google News corpus (vocabulary capped at the 1M most frequent words) with DistBelief — 50–100 model replicas, asynchronous mini-batch gradient descent, Adagrad — a 1000-dimensional skip-gram becomes the paper's headline model.
  • Beyond analogies: on the Microsoft Sentence Completion Challenge, skip-gram alone scores 48.0%, and mixed with RNNLM reaches 58.9% — a new state of the art at the time.

Edges of this paper

  • Not in here: negative sampling. This paper's output is an exact (hierarchical) softmax. Negative sampling, frequent-word subsampling and phrase vectors (New York as one token) arrive in the follow-up, Distributed Representations of Words and Phrases (NIPS 2013) — most "word2vec" explainers describe that paper, not this one.
  • The count-based contrast: GloVe (2014) reaches similar geometry from global co-occurrence counts instead of a sliding classifier — the Step's Extra lane.
  • The through-line to LLMs: every model later in the Plan starts with an embedding layer. It is trained jointly with the network rather than on its own, but it rests on exactly this paper's assumption — meaning is a position in vector space, and relations are directions in it.
